When recurring production changes the math

Formlabs describes Tesla’s glass-handling shim application at roughly 10,000–20,000 parts per month over a six-month need. Its account says the lower printing cost with Fuse X1 could remove the reason to invest in an injection mold for that part. This is a specific manufacturer-reported example, rather than a universal volume threshold.

Precision comes from the part and process evidence

Define the critical dimensions, datum scheme, material, surface finish, fit, loading, and operating environment. Request representative sample parts and inspection results before treating a process as interchangeable with molding.

Formlabs’ dimensional study evaluates Nylon 12 on the Fuse 1+ 30W, including part position and repeated builds. That evidence is useful context, but it is not a tolerance guarantee for every Fuse X1 part, material, or build arrangement. Review accuracy against nominal dimensions separately from repeatability.

Technical reference: Measuring SLS dimensional accuracy and print repeatability.

Ask for comparable production quotes

  • Same drawing revision, material requirements, quantity, and delivery cadence.
  • Tooling ownership, design-change costs, minimum orders, and replacement tooling.
  • Finishing, inspection, packing, freight, rejects, and acceptance criteria.
  • Actual available capacity, lead time, service support, and recovery plan.

Choose the process for the job

Evaluate printing for changing designs, mixed batches, bridge production, and shorter runs. Evaluate molding when the design and demand are stable enough to spread tooling costs across the required volume. Let quotes and measured samples decide the crossover.
